Driver workflow

A DVIR app built around how your fleet works

Keep the inspection action simple and make reported defects visible to the team responsible for them.

A driver vehicle inspection report should be easy to complete at the end of a workday or when a defect is found. A custom mobile app can guide the driver through the fleet’s inspection workflow, identify the power unit, capture a description and photo, and send the report to dispatch or maintenance without relying on a text message or a paper form.

The office view matters just as much. A report needs an owner, a review status and a way to record what was repaired. Managers should be able to find reports by vehicle, driver and date, see which issues are still open, and keep the repair record connected to the report. That makes the process easier to manage across shifts and vehicle changes.

The app can include vehicle and trailer selection, odometer entry, defect categories, photo capture, digital acknowledgements, notifications and an audit trail. Which fields are required depends on the carrier’s operation and the applicable rules; they should be decided before the interface is designed.

  • Power-unit and trailer identification
  • Configurable inspection prompts
  • Defect details with optional photos
  • Driver and supervisor acknowledgement
  • Maintenance assignment and repair notes
  • Searchable reports and export
US regulation context

Build around the actual DVIR rule

A US implementation must reflect the carrier type and the current federal and state requirements.

FMCSA’s 49 CFR 396.11 governs driver vehicle inspection reports. The federal rule generally requires a written report when a driver discovers or is informed of a defect or deficiency that could affect safe operation or cause a breakdown. FMCSA’s rule change removed the no-defect report requirement for most non-passenger interstate commercial motor vehicle drivers, while passenger-carrying vehicles remain an exception.

That distinction matters to product design. A fleet may still choose to record a completed no-defect check for its own operational process, but a website or app should not describe that choice as a universal federal requirement. The report logic can be configured to the carrier’s policy and the regulations that apply to its vehicles and routes.

The system can keep a report history, track whether a defect was reviewed and record the repair certification where the carrier requires it. The responsible carrier should confirm report fields, retention and signature requirements with its compliance lead or counsel before launch.

Product design

Connect the driver report to maintenance follow-up

The useful part of digitising a report is what happens after a defect is submitted.

A well-designed flow lets the driver identify the vehicle, record the defect in plain language, attach a photo if useful and submit it to the person who can act. The office can classify urgency, assign a repair, note when the vehicle can return to service and close the issue with a record of the action taken.

Drivers may use different tractors or trailers during a shift, so a DVIR app should make equipment selection explicit and preserve the report against the correct vehicle record. If a driver discovers a new issue later, the app should make it clear how to create another report instead of editing away the original history.

For larger operations, the app can connect to dispatch, maintenance software, fuel or telematics data. These integrations need a defined system of record and clear conflict handling. We scope those details before promising a sync so the delivered app has reliable, understandable behaviour.

Scope and delivery

Choose a first release that solves one clear problem

A focused first release gives drivers and maintenance staff a chance to shape the workflow before more modules are added.

Discovery starts with your current inspection form, driver hand-off, defect escalation and repair sign-off. We identify what varies by vehicle type, who needs notifications, what records must be retained, and what happens when a driver has no signal. This produces a feature list and a practical first-release boundary.

The proposal defines the mobile app, supervisor view, user roles, integrations, hosting, data ownership and delivery milestones. You see working software on a staging link as features are built, and your team can review it with real operating scenarios before release.

A custom build makes sense when existing products cannot support your reporting, integration or ownership needs. If a standard DVIR product already fits and costs less to operate, it may be the simpler choice. We can help compare the workflows before committing to a build.
